# CHAPTER 1: THE TWILIGHT OF MEDIEVAL BIHAR

## The Decline of Imperial Authority

The year 1526 marks a conventional starting point for this history, with the Battle of Panipat and Babur's establishment of Mughal power in northern India. But to understand what Mughal rule meant for Bihar, we must first comprehend what existed before—an intricate patchwork of local powers that had emerged from the disintegration of the Delhi Sultanate's authority in the preceding century.

The Delhi Sultanate, which had exercised varying degrees of control over Bihar since the 12th century, entered a period of terminal decline in the early 16th century. The Lodhi dynasty, which ruled from Delhi, found its authority increasingly challenged by provincial governors who behaved as independent rulers. In Bihar, the governors appointed by Delhi had gradually transformed themselves into autonomous powers, recognizing the Sultan in name only while exercising full sovereignty in practice.

This fragmentation was not unique to Bihar; it characterized much of northern India in the period immediately preceding Mughal expansion. What made Bihar distinctive was its geographical position at the intersection of several power centers—Delhi to the west, Bengal to the east, and Orissa to the south—and its relative wealth derived from fertile agricultural land and strategic trade routes along the Ganges.

## Local Rajput Kingdoms and Zamindari Powers

As central authority weakened, local power structures came to the fore. The most significant of these were the Rajput chieftaincies and zamindari estates that controlled substantial territories and commanded military forces. These were not merely tax collectors; they were rulers in all but name, exercising judicial authority, maintaining armies, and conducting diplomacy with neighboring powers.

### The Ujjainiya Rajputs of Bhojpur

The Ujjainiya Rajputs, who controlled much of the Bhojpur region, exemplify the emergence of local powers during this period. Claiming descent from the Paramara dynasty of Malwa, the Ujjainiyas had established themselves as the dominant power in the region between the Ganges and the Son rivers. Under their leader, the Bhojpur raja exercised authority over a substantial territory and maintained a military force capable of resisting external encroachments.

The Ujjainiyas were not simply military adventurers; they represented a pattern of state formation that would characterize much of Bihar's history. Their power rested on a combination of military prowess, control of land, and the ability to extract surplus from agricultural production. They maintained elaborate administrative structures, patronized religious institutions, and established marriage alliances with other Rajput clans to secure their position.

### The Chero and Other Tribal Kingdoms

In the southern and eastern parts of Bihar, particularly in the region that would later become the Chota Nagpur plateau, tribal communities maintained their independence through a combination of geographical isolation and military resistance. The Chero dynasty, which ruled in what is now the Palamau region, had successfully resisted integration into the larger empires of the period.

The Chero kingdoms, like their Rajput counterparts, possessed their own administrative systems, maintained armies, and conducted trade with neighboring regions. Their survival into the Mughal period and beyond demonstrates the limitations of imperial power in certain geographical contexts and the resilience of local political traditions.

## The Economy of Late Medieval Bihar

The political fragmentation of the period did not necessarily mean economic decline. In many respects, the 15th and early 16th centuries saw significant economic activity in Bihar, driven by agricultural productivity, trade along the Ganges, and the production of specialized crafts.

### Agriculture and Land Use

Bihar's economy remained fundamentally agricultural, with the fertile plains of the Ganges and its tributaries supporting intensive cultivation of rice, wheat, and other crops. The zamindari system, which would become fully developed under the Mughals and the British, had its origins in this period. Local chieftains and powerful families exercised control over land and extracted rent from cultivators, creating a hierarchical but productive agricultural system.

The region's agricultural wealth was enhanced by irrigation works, many of which dated back to ancient times. The maintenance and expansion of these systems required coordination and investment, which the local powers of the period were able to provide.

### Trade and Commerce

Bihar's location on the Ganges made it a crucial node in trade networks connecting the Gangetic plain with Bengal, Nepal, and beyond. The river itself served as the primary artery of commerce, with boats carrying goods from Patna to major centers like Varanasi, Allahabad, and eventually Calcutta.

The major commodities traded through Bihar included:
- Agricultural products, particularly rice and wheat
- Salt, which was produced in the region or imported from coastal areas
- Textiles, including cotton cloth and specialized products
- Metals and metalwork
- Horses, which were imported from central Asia and the northwest

The towns of Patna, Hajipur, and other river ports served as centers of commercial activity, with merchant communities from various parts of India establishing themselves in these centers.

## The Arrival of the Mughals

### Babur's Invasion and the Battle of Panipat

When Babur defeated Ibrahim Lodhi at Panipat in April 1526, he initiated a process that would fundamentally transform the political landscape of northern India. The Mughal conquest brought a new level of centralized authority, administrative sophistication, and military organization to regions that had experienced political fragmentation for decades.

However, the establishment of Mughal authority in Bihar did not occur immediately or without resistance. Babur's initial focus was on consolidating his hold over Delhi and the Punjab, and it was left to his successors to extend Mughal power into the eastern regions.

### Humayun's Campaigns and the Annexation of Bihar

It was under Humayun, Babur's son, that Bihar was formally incorporated into the Mughal Empire. The campaigns of the late 1530s brought Bihar under direct Mughal administration, establishing the foundation for over two centuries of Mughal rule.

The annexation of Bihar served multiple strategic purposes for the Mughals. It provided:
- Agricultural revenue to support the imperial treasury
- A base for further expansion into Bengal
- Control of trade routes along the Ganges
- Military resources in the form of troops and supplies

The establishment of Mughal authority was not merely a military matter; it required the creation of administrative structures, the appointment of officials, and the integration of local elites into the imperial system.

## The Afghan Challenge: Sher Shah Suri's Origins

### The Rise of Afghan Power in Bihar

The Mughal conquest did not go unchallenged. The period from 1540 to 1555 saw the temporary displacement of Mughal power by the Sur dynasty, founded by Sher Shah Suri. The rise of Sher Shah from a minor jagirdar in Bihar to the ruler of much of northern India is one of the most remarkable stories in Indian history, and its origins lie in the specific conditions of Bihar in the early 16th century.

Sher Shah's success was not merely a personal achievement; it reflected the strength of Afghan military traditions, the weaknesses of early Mughal administration, and the opportunities created by the political fragmentation of the period. The Afghans of Bihar, many of whom had served under the Lodhi sultans of Delhi, represented a formidable military force that had not been fully integrated into the Mughal system.

### The Making of a King: Farid Khan's Early Years

Born as Farid Khan around 1486 in the Sasaram region of modern Bihar, Sher Shah's early life exemplifies the possibilities for social mobility in the turbulent conditions of the period. His father, Hasan Khan, held a jagir (land grant) in the region, but the family's position was modest compared to the great zamindars and chieftains of the area.

Farid Khan's rise to power involved service under various masters, military exploits, and the gradual accumulation of territory and followers. The title "Sher Khan"—"Tiger Lord"—was earned through his military prowess, reportedly for killing a tiger single-handedly. This combination of personal courage and administrative ability would prove decisive in his subsequent career.

## Religious and Cultural Life

### Hindu-Muslim Relations

The period preceding Mughal consolidation was characterized by complex patterns of Hindu-Muslim interaction. While the ruling elites in Delhi and the provincial centers were predominantly Muslim, the vast majority of the population remained Hindu. Religious boundaries were porous, with syncretic practices, shared sacred spaces, and mutual cultural influences characterizing everyday life.

The Sufi tradition, which had deep roots in Bihar, played a particularly important role in shaping religious interactions. Sufi shrines attracted devotees from all communities, and Sufi teachers often served as mediators in local disputes. The syncretic religious culture that would be celebrated (and sometimes condemned) in later periods had its foundations in the medieval centuries.

### The Bhakti Movement

The 15th and 16th centuries also witnessed the flourishing of the Bhakti movement in Bihar and neighboring regions. This devotional tradition, which emphasized personal devotion to a chosen deity over ritual and caste hierarchy, attracted followers from all social backgrounds and challenged established religious authorities.

The Bhakti saints of the period composed works in vernacular languages, making religious teachings accessible to ordinary people. The use of regional languages—Magahi, Bhojpuri, Maithili—reflected and reinforced the development of linguistic identities that would persist into modern times.

## The Legacy of Fragmentation

The period of political fragmentation left lasting marks on Bihar's history. The local powers that emerged during the decline of Delhi—Rajput chieftains, tribal kingdoms, Afghan military commanders—would continue to exercise influence throughout the Mughal period and beyond. Many of the zamindari estates that would become so significant under British rule had their origins in this earlier period.

More importantly, the experience of relative autonomy created expectations and traditions of local self-governance that would shape responses to subsequent periods of centralized rule. The tension between local autonomy and imperial authority—a tension that would characterize much of Bihar's history—has its roots in the medieval centuries.

The arrival of the Mughals would bring new patterns of administration, new forms of cultural patronage, and new modes of economic extraction. But it would not erase the foundations laid in the preceding period. The history of Bihar under Mughal rule must be understood as a dialogue between imperial designs and local realities, between the ambitions of conquerors and the resistance (and accommodation) of the conquered.

**Analytical Commentary**

The period from 1500 to the full establishment of Mughal authority represents a crucial transition in Bihar's history. The fragmentation of imperial power created space for local actors—Rajput chieftains, Afghan commanders, tribal kingdoms—to exercise unprecedented autonomy. This was not simply political chaos but a period of state formation, as local powers developed administrative structures, military capabilities, and territorial control.

The significance of this period lies not only in what happened but in what it established for the future. The zamindari system, the tension between local autonomy and central authority, the complex patterns of Hindu-Muslim relations—all these elements of later Bihar history have their roots in the medieval centuries. Understanding this foundation is essential for comprehending what followed.

Sher Shah Suri's rise from obscurity to empire is often presented as a personal success story, and it certainly was that. But it was also a symptom of broader patterns: the military potential of Afghan communities, the opportunities created by Mughal weakness, and the possibilities for social mobility in a period of political flux. Sher Shah's subsequent administrative reforms, which would influence Mughal and even British governance, drew upon the practical experience gained in the conditions of medieval Bihar.

The Mughal conquest, when it finally came, did not simply replace one ruling class with another; it created a new system of relationships between center and periphery, between imperial authority and local power. The history of Bihar under Mughal rule is the story of how these relationships were negotiated, contested, and ultimately transformed.
