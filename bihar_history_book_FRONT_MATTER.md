## PREFACE

### Why This Book, Why Now

Growing up in Banka, in the heart of Bihar, I learned early that our history was something to be whispered rather than proclaimed. In school textbooks, Bihar appeared as a footnote—famous only for its poverty, its caste conflicts, its backwardness. The magnificent empires that rose from our soil, the revolutionary movements that began in our fields, the literary traditions that flourished in our languages—all seemed to have been swallowed by a collective amnesia that afflicted both the nation and our own people.

I remember walking through the ruins of ancient universities in Nalanda, standing before the grand tomb of Sher Shah Suri in Sasaram, and visiting the humble mud huts of Champaran where Mahatma Gandhi first tested the power of satyagraha. Each site told a story of glory and tragedy, of resistance and resilience, of a land that had been both the cradle of empires and the victim of exploitation. Yet these stories were absent from the history books I studied, absent from the national narrative, absent even from the consciousness of many Biharis themselves.

This book is my attempt to reclaim those lost centuries—to write the history of Bihar from 1500 to the present day with the depth, nuance, and critical analysis that this ancient land deserves. It is not merely a chronicle of events but an interrogation of how power operates, how societies transform, and how historical memory is constructed and erased.

### The Historian's Burden

Writing this book has been both a scholarly endeavor and a personal journey. As a historian trained in [author's credentials to be added], I have approached this work with rigorous attention to archival sources, comparative analysis, and theoretical frameworks. But I have also written it as a son of Bihar, with all the passion, prejudice, and partiality that such an identity entails.

This dual position—insider and outsider, participant and observer—presents both opportunities and challenges. I have access to oral histories, folk traditions, and cultural knowledge that might elude a foreign scholar. Yet I must also guard against the temptations of romanticization, the urge to paint a picture of unbroken glory, the desire to transform historical analysis into nationalist myth-making.

I have tried to navigate this tension by adhering to certain principles:

1. **Source Criticism**: Every major claim in this book is supported by primary sources, whether archival documents, contemporary chronicles, or archaeological evidence. Where sources are contradictory or unclear, I have said so.

2. **Comparative Analysis**: Bihar's history cannot be understood in isolation. I have consistently compared developments in Bihar with those in Bengal, Uttar Pradesh, and South India to identify patterns of similarity and difference.

3. **Structural Analysis**: Events do not occur in a vacuum. I have tried to analyze the underlying economic, social, and political structures that shaped historical outcomes.

4. **Voice of the Subaltern**: History written from the perspective of elites tells only half the story. I have sought to recover the voices of peasants, workers, women, and marginalized communities wherever possible.

### Scope and Structure

This book covers five centuries of Bihar's history, from the dawn of Mughal power in 1526 to the present day in 2026. This period encompasses:

- The medieval period under the Mughals and Afghan rulers (1526-1757)
- The establishment of British colonial rule and the creation of the Zamindari system (1757-1857)
- The Revolt of 1857 and its aftermath (1857-1885)
- The nationalist movement and the road to independence (1885-1947)
- The post-independence period, including the creation of Jharkhand (1947-2026)

The book is divided into twenty-five chapters, organized thematically and chronologically. Each chapter combines narrative history with analytical commentary, examining not just what happened but why it happened and what it meant.

### A Note on Terminology

Historical writing inevitably involves choices about terminology, and these choices carry political weight. I have used the term "British colonial rule" rather than "British Raj" to emphasize the exploitative nature of the relationship. I have referred to the events of 1857 as the "Revolt" or "Uprising" rather than the "Mutiny" to acknowledge the widespread participation of civilians and the political nature of the resistance.

I have used "Bihar" throughout to refer to the region, even when discussing periods before the formal creation of the province in 1912. While anachronistic, this usage reflects the geographical and cultural continuity of the region.

### A Final Reflection

History is not merely the past. It is the foundation upon which the present is built and the lens through which the future is imagined. For too long, Bihar has been defined by others—by colonial administrators who saw it as a source of revenue, by nationalist leaders who saw it as a staging ground for struggle, by contemporary commentators who see it only as a symbol of backwardness.

It is time for Biharis to reclaim their own story—to write a history that acknowledges both our achievements and our failures, that celebrates our resilience without ignoring our oppression, that honors our traditions while embracing the possibilities of change.

This book is a contribution to that project. It is offered not as the final word but as an opening of a conversation—a conversation about who we were, who we are, and who we might become.
